Worried it might become a lost film, Jane decided to scour auction sites, search every library catalog she could think of, and even enlist some of her online acquaintances to find a copy. Private trackers are essentially the speakeasies of the online piracy world. Promoted primarily by word of mouth, they rely on secrecy, exclusivity, and strict community rules to shield everything that’s going on behind closed doors from the public’s prying eyes. What unites these communities is that they all use BitTorrent to trade media among like-minded file sharers. But their focuses range widely, from general-purpose trackers with catalogs so broad that they make Netflix look like a niche service to the many specialized communities for categories like Chinese movies, motorsports, visual effects tools, e-learning courses, and Japanese porn. Private trackers are known for massive and meticulously organized catalogs, which they owe to users like Jane, who, like every other pirate I talked to for this story, opted to be identified by a pseudonym due to the terabytes of copyrighted data they’ve been sharing. These are people willing to go the extra mile to find long-forgotten movies, rare concert recordings, or out-of-print books. “Many private tracker users are fully consumed with archive fever,” she says.
At first, Jane had no luck finding a physical copy of that elusive documentary, which includes interviews with Steve Jobs and Julia Child. Nobody was selling it on eBay. Even getting access to that first server came with some hurdles. Private tracker websites rarely have freely accessible sign-up forms. Some only open their doors to new users every few weeks or months, while others use special vetting processes to make sure applicants are a good fit. Sometimes this means that applicants are required to prove they’re in good standing on other trackers. Sometimes they need to take screenshots of their personal media collections to show they’re actually interested in what the tracker they’re applying to is all about. Not every private tracker has these kinds of interviews, but like Jane’s first server, most are big on rules. Users are generally required to keep up a certain upload-to-download ratio, with many sites demanding that they contribute as much bandwidth as they consume. (The exact rules and requirements vary, and many trackers also offer some “freeleech” content — files that can be downloaded without negatively impacting a user’s ratio.) Quickly downloading a file without sharing it is also frowned upon, as is the unvetted distribution of invites to outsiders.
Users who fall below certain metrics often find their download privileges curtailed, and especially egregious offenses result in instant bans. “Invite selling is considered to be a major breach of security,” says Odel. Doing so will not only get you kicked from a server, it will also tarnish your reputation elsewhere. “Staff of the tracker you did it on will typically communicate with the staff of some other trackers, which can result in you getting banned from those places as well,” Odel explains.
If you do follow the rules and share generously, you have a chance to work your way up the ranks, which then opens the doors to other trackers. That alone, and the requirement to keep up a healthy upload-to-download ratio, can lead to people downloading and then redistributing (also known as “seeding” in tracker lingo) files just to maintain their stats. “Some places will require you to seed [a certain amount of] terabytes before you get access to a certain rank,” says Odel. “This can result in a lot of downloading of stuff I wouldn’t ever consume otherwise, just to pump up that number.”

This is called “racing” in tracker lingo, and it can help improve one’s ratio; anyone looking to download that file later will get it from those early downloaders. Especially committed users even rent a dedicated seedbox — a private server sitting in some remote data center that is only used for downloading and then seeding torrents.

Private BitTorrent trackers emerged soon after BitTorrent itself was created 25 years ago. This was at the height of the file-sharing wars, when Hollywood and the major record labels were taking one peer-to-peer network operator after another to court. Entertainment industry lawsuits resulted in the shutdown of Napster in 2001, Audiogalaxy in 2002, and Kazaa in 2006.
Those closures drove millions of file sharers to embrace BitTorrent, which in turn put torrent sites into the crosshairs of rightsholders and their lawyers. Suprnova, a public torrent site frequented by millions of users, shut down amid legal pressure at the end of 2004. And in 2006, authorities raided The Pirate Bay.
Some torrent site admins responded to the increasing legal pressure by simply pulling up a drawbridge to their piracy communities. Instead of letting just anyone download torrents, they required users to register, and in some cases only admitted new users that had gotten an invite from a trusted community member.

The curation happening in these private communities is very much a group effort. Many private trackers offer their members a chance to request files they want others to upload, complete with bounties (download credits that you would otherwise have to upload other files for) for the first uploader. The rarer the file, the bigger the bounty. And the bigger the bounty, the more frenzied the hunt for that elusive file.

One famous quest for such a bounty unfolded in 2013, when a user of the since-shuttered What.CD server put out a request for “The Ocean Full of Bowling Balls,” a story from Catcher in the Rye author J.D. For a long time, the only known copy of “The Ocean Full of Bowling Balls” resided in Princeton University’s Firestone Library, where visitors could read but not copy the document.
The intrigue — Salinger didn’t publish a single work for the last 45 years of his life — combined with the fact that there clearly was at least one copy out there set off a frenzy among What.CD users, with the bounty reportedly swelling to a massive 6TB. Some came up with schemes to memorize every word of the story while visiting Princeton’s library, while others scoured the Earth for other copies.

Years later, one of those 25 copies showed up on eBay, where the What.CD uploader quickly snagged it.
The story of this unlikely act of renegade archiving quickly made headlines worldwide, and to this day is legendary in the private tracker community. Odel told me he doesn’t personally partake in these kinds of media hunts. “But I’ve seen some things which I’d classify as ‘important’ be recovered this way,” he says. “Piracy does happen to be pretty good at preservation.”
UC Berkeley professor Abigail De Kosnik, who has investigated private trackers as part of her research on rogue online archives, agrees. “A great deal of media released today will persist via private servers, much like how a lot of silent films we have today were found as reels stored in people’s attics,” she says. “I think that private torrent trackers, which make possible [to download] many art films, indie films, smaller-budget films, films from many countries, will play a large part in constituting future archives of 20th and 21st century cultural productions.”
Copyright holders, unsurprisingly, often hold less favorable views of the private tracker community. “Private trackers are part of a broader piracy ecosystem,” says Larissa Knapp, executive vice president and chief content protection officer of the Motion Picture Association (MPA). “Content that first appears within a closed community can quickly migrate to streaming sites, cyberlockers, IPTV services, and other unauthorized platforms, significantly amplifying its reach and impact.”
Rightsholder groups have repeatedly gone after private trackers and their administrators with varying levels of success. Oink’s Pink Palace, the popular private music tracker, shut down in 2007 following raids in the Netherlands and the UK. Oink admin Alan Ellis was arrested and charged with conspiracy to defraud as part of the enforcement actions, but was later found not guilty in court. Shuttering the tracker also did little to stop file sharing. Oink was quickly succeeded by What.CD, which remained online for nearly a decade until finally closing down in 2016 following a raid on its data center.
These days, private file sharing communities aren’t as often in the news as they were a decade ago. Part of that is simply a matter of priority. Private trackers often cap the number of registered users; some of the most coveted communities have only 40,000 or fewer active members. Public sites like The Pirate Bay, on the other hand, still attract millions of file sharers, making it easier for commercial copyright enforcers to send out tens of thousands of legal notices to end users at a time — a practice that’s especially common in Europe.
Still, groups like the MPA, and its ACE anti-piracy arm, haven’t given up on going after private file sharing communities and their admins. “Private trackers are not beyond enforcement reach,”                 Knapp says. “Closed access can make an investigation more difficult, but it does not eliminate the legal or operational exposure for the people who run them. In my years in federal law enforcement, I learned that anonymity is rarely as complete as people believe. A private community is not a safe harbor. Every operation, no matter how closed, still depends on people, hosting and domain services, communications tools, and usually some way to move money. Each of those is a connection, and connections are what investigators follow.”

The fast-moving copyright battle around AI adds further ambiguity to this debate. “Now that the fastest-growing corner of the knowledge industries relies on mass piracy — copying huge amounts of data that they download for free — I think that the anti-piracy rhetoric of the past quarter-century is looking a bit outdated,” says De Kosnik. “If anything, AI [companies] are anti-archival because they strip away attribution from the creators, whereas private trackers are intensely accurate in providing details about the source material. Still, some of the users I spoke to were also acutely aware that it’s hard for them to take the moral high road. “It may genuinely hurt smaller creators,” Odel admits. “I’m not a fan of the people who try to advocate for it being moral or anything. I tend to justify my own piracy under the lense of ‘I wouldn’t be watching x otherwise.’ Whether that would be true assuming I was suddenly unable to pirate anything, I do not know.”
Not everyone is as conflicted about using private trackers. “I own it, it’s piracy. I torrent this content because I find it very convenient and don’t want to pay for every audiobook, TV episode, movie, etc.,” says Marvin.
Marvin tells me he originally got into piracy for financial reasons. “It was a habit that started with me growing up poor,” he says. Nowadays, that’s less of an issue for him, and he does spend money on some content, including books and video games.
“But I am a pirate at heart,” Marvin acknowledges. “This is paid content that I am getting for free. I’ve never paid for a subscription to a major streamer, and at this point, I don’t see why I ever would.”
